HomeEquity Bank sells the CHIP Reverse Mortgage, a product that had spent its whole life with the stigma of last resort. Meanwhile roughly a trillion dollars of Canadian equity sits in boomer-owned houses, scheduled to reach Gen X and millennials through inheritance.

Gifting behaviour already exists. Parents fund down payments by refinancing, but the bank had no way to meet that behaviour in the moment, because its application journey was manual, slow, and built for a customer in distress rather than a parent doing something generous on purpose.

We built HomeBridge as a separate division inside HomeEquity Bank with its own brand, team and governance. I joined as design lead, working with the customer success organization and its VP, alongside the COO and CTO's teams, and presenting to a venture board that included HEB's CEO, CFO and board representation.

The investment case stated roughly 70% of build capital went to rebuilding the origination journey and the stack underneath it, which made design the majority of the venture's spend. And under value levers, the case stated the value of the new origination journey to the core business—but didn’t model it.

## What I did

### Made the parent the customer

The venture started with the hypothesis of young person can't afford a house, so build for the young person and let them bring it to their parents. We had the customer backwards, and a fair bit of thinking was already stacked on top of that assumption.

The parent has the equity and makes the decision. The parent also has to be the one who suggests it. Children who open the conversation feel like they're begging, or being selfish, or putting their parents' retirement at risk, and plenty of them would rather not buy a house than feel that way. They felt the early-inheritance framing was manipulative, because it asks them to  think about their parents dying for a condo. Not fun.

So I rebuilt the service around the parent as customer of record, with the family as participant. Both segments stayed in the funnel and the adult child remained a real lead source. In-market data agreed. The right parent was harder to find but converted better as the decision maker, while leads that started with the child fell away at the exact point the child had to go home and raise it. 

The product launched speaking to homeowners 55+ about a living legacy.

### Unblocked the build so I could answer the harder question

As in most build phases, the pressure on a designer is to produce mockups. Product and engineering had a joruney to build and wanted screens, and I wanted time to go sit with families, because marketing and sales had nothing to work with until somebody understood how this conversation actually happens at the kitchen table.

So I built a design system with specifications and logic flows that product and engineering could keep building against, with real components and defined behaviour instead of waiting on mockups. This understandably kept the build moving and bought me time and space to run the research.

I designed and facilitated in-depth interviews with brokers, with parents whose children were house-hunting, and with a mix of children hunting for a home and children who had already taken gifting help. This research helped frame marketing and product decisions, as well as spawn the need for family communication research.

To address this, I designed and facilitated a two-hour generative workshop with four parent and adult-child pairs, recruited against conversation orientation and conformity orientation so we would see closed, high-conforimty families rather than only the articulate ones who really really enjoy talking about money. Four pairs is a small sample, and the findings went out framed as directional and flagged for testing at scale. It really is difficult to recruit for something like this.

Nine insights and three opportunity areas came out of it, and they went straight into the parts of the business that interact with customers. Marketing absorbed most of it into messaging and sales scripts and rebuilt its approach around the tension between a child's independence and a parent's drive to provide.

### Drew the line the business adopted

The research also handed us something I didn't love having.

We now knew which conversations children avoid, which framings make parents feel disrespected, and which family structures produce a parent who will say yes to almost anything their child asks. A lender can use all of that to close loans. We were a lender, and we were behind on originations.

I took the position to the venture board that HomeBridge exists to support a financial decision, not to resolve or exploit family dynamics, and that a parent must never arrive at the decision feeling ambushed. The business adopted it. The sales approach couldn't be built to route around a hesitant parent. Lead engagement had to work through the parent instead of past them. Product ideas that would have converted well never got built, and I'm the reason they didn't. It had as much to do with ethics as it did with regulations.

### Built the flow, and the role that carries the research

Anyway, insights die in decks. So we hired a customer success representative and trained her to surface who actually makes the financial decisions, whether the adult child holds outsized influence over the parent's money, whether the parent is deciding emotionally. The question set we provided qualified the lead and flagged potential undue influence before the file could reach underwriting. The role persists on the go-forward team as the HomeBridge Advisor.

Based on all this research and observation, I designed the origination journey end to end as a service blueprint, product design, user testing, handoffs and timing. We built the sales funnel against real causes of dropoff, including leads arriving without usable contact details, unqualified leads reaching a consultation, and applications missing what was needed to determine eligibility. 

I also designed the independent legal advice flow, which is the moment a reverse mortgage most often stalls. A family with momentum gets told to go find a lawyer. I specified it as a heavy backend with a deliberately thin front end, so the borrower sees a few plain steps while coordination, document handling and verification sit behind the interface. It was descoped under launch pressure and returned to the roadmap, with the flow specified for the bank to finish.

The MVP shipped July 16, 2024, live in the broker channel with eight business development managers. The first loan came through the flow shortly after, from a broker who had never sent HEB a deal.

## What changed

**HomeEquity Bank now runs this new origination flow for every loan it originates.** It performed well enough on lead conversion and customer experience that the bank rolled it out across the entire organization, for all leads, rather than keeping it inside the venture. Once it replaced the manual process, originations grew 60% across the bank's products. Reusability by the core bank was written into the venture's incubation objectives, a value lever we executed against.

**HomeBridge launched publicly in February 2025 and is still an offering**, positioned to homeowners 55+ establishing a living legacy. The bank has since reported a 15.5% increase in customers using home equity for gifting, and extended the gifting journey across its wider business in July 2025.

**Six people took the division forward**, including the managing director, product and technology leadership, go-to-market, operations, and the advisor role carrying the family-dynamics work.

**The standalone division no longer exists.** HEB reorganized, dissolved HomeBridge and absorbed its functions into the core bank. The constraints that did it in were the usual enterprise ones rather than product ones like limited decision autonomy, slow risk processes, and underutilized distribution channels. The HomeBridge brand was like a car… the origination system was the engine, and it's still running.

## What I learned

The thing a client asks you to build and the thing that outlives you are frequently different things, and both are usually visible on day one if you read the business case closely enough. The excluded line items are where the value tends to sit, and I love to read between the lines.
